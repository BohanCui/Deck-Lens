# Mode: Government / Institution
**Use for:** Formal submissions to government bodies, public institutions, regulatory authorities, or state-affiliated organizations

This audience values process legitimacy, objective evidence, operational stability, and accountability. They are risk-averse by nature. Every claim must be traceable. Every solution must be verifiable. Innovation is welcome only when it comes with guardrails.

The goal is not to impress — it is to satisfy the review process and earn institutional trust.

---

## The 9-Beat Government Structure

| # | Beat | Purpose |
|---|------|---------|
| 1 | **Requirement Source** | Where did this need come from? Cite the policy directive, leadership mandate, regulation, or work plan that makes this project necessary. Establish institutional legitimacy before anything else. |
| 2 | **Current Situation** | Objective description of the status quo. Data-backed. No editorializing. What is the current state of the relevant system, process, or service? |
| 3 | **Problem Analysis** | What specific problems exist? Quantify the impact where possible: efficiency loss, error rate, resource waste, compliance risk. Trace problems to their root causes. |
| 4 | **Solution Overview** | Describe the proposed solution at a high level. Emphasize that it is appropriate in scope, aligned with relevant standards, and technically sound. Avoid novelty for its own sake — frame innovation in terms of necessity and reliability. |
| 5 | **Implementation Plan** | Timeline, phases, responsible parties, resource requirements. Show that execution is planned and feasible. Include dependencies and key milestones. |
| 6 | **Functional Description** | What does the system or solution do? Cover core functions, operational scenarios, and user-facing capabilities. Demonstrate that it has been or can be validated before full deployment. |
| 7 | **Application Outcomes** | How will success be measured? Define concrete, verifiable indicators: response time, error reduction, user adoption, cost savings. Results should be auditable. |
| 8 | **Risk Assessment & Control** | Identify key risks (technical, operational, compliance, security). For each risk, state the likelihood, potential impact, and specific mitigation measure. Do not omit risks — acknowledging them builds credibility. |
| 9 | **Safeguards & Conclusion** | What institutional support, oversight mechanisms, and fallback procedures are in place? Close with a clear summary of the project's value, its alignment with institutional goals, and a confident but measured recommendation. |

## Slide Count Guidance

| Beat | Slides |
|------|--------|
| 1 Requirement Source | 1 |
| 2 Current Situation | 1–2 |
| 3 Problem Analysis | 1–2 |
| 4 Solution Overview | 2 |
| 5 Implementation Plan | 1–2 |
| 6 Functional Description | 2–3 |
| 7 Application Outcomes | 1–2 |
| 8 Risk Assessment | 1–2 |
| 9 Safeguards & Conclusion | 1 |
| **Total** | **~13–18 slides** |

## Tone Notes
- Formal, measured, institutional — no slogans, no enthusiasm performance
- Every claim should be traceable to a source, policy, or data point
- Avoid superlatives ("revolutionary", "world-class") — use factual descriptors instead
- Risk slides are not a weakness — omitting them is
- Language should read like a policy document, not a pitch deck
- If there are appendices (technical specs, regulatory citations, org charts), attach them after the main body
- The conclusion should close with institutional alignment: how this project serves the organization's mandate, not just its efficiency
